Waterfall at the Speed of AI

  • DATE: November 25, 2026
  • AUTHOR: Chris Amato
  • CATEGORY: Process
Generative AI is transforming how quickly we build software. It may also be reviving some of our oldest mistakes.

I've been developing software for more than twenty years, and the productivity gains from working with generative AI are unlike anything I've experienced. A small group can take a stack of imagined requirements and have something that runs in a few days. Someone raises a contradiction, and it can be gone before the meeting ends. From the outside this looks like Agile. The requirements are moving. The software is changing. Feedback is constant.

I didn't expect the uneasy part. The iteration is real. I'm no longer sure it always leaves the specification.

Waterfall didn't fall out of favor merely because it was slow. We do not know enough at the start to specify the right system. People discover needs they could not name in a meeting. Features that looked necessary turn out to be unused. Agile tried to shorten the distance between building something, putting it into use, and deciding what to build next. These tools collapse a different distance: the one between an idea and a convincing version of that idea.

We can iterate constantly and still be refining a solution we assumed at the beginning.

Two kinds of iteration

This is the distinction I keep coming back to.

One kind of iteration refines a specification. People describe how they believe a system should behave. A prototype makes that description runnable. They inspect it and adjust the description. The model gets more precise, and usually more complete. The feedback is real, and it mostly confirms or elaborates what the group already expected.

The other kind tests an idea against reality. Someone who was not in the room tries to get something done. The task is realistic enough that the premise can fail. What they actually do reveals bottlenecks, misunderstandings, and uses nobody wrote down. A prototype can do this work. It does not have to be in production. Real users, a realistic task, and permission for the whole approach to be rejected are enough to learn something you did not arrive with.

Both move quickly. Both produce feedback. They do not produce the same knowledge.

I have watched the first kind run for dozens of cycles and never touch the assumption underneath: that this is the system that should exist. What comes out is a more elaborate, internally consistent version of the original guess. The tools make that kind of iteration so productive that it is easy to call it Agile. The speed is in the building. The philosophy can still be "decide the solution, then construct it." The construction just no longer takes long enough to hurt.

A convincing prototype borrows authority

A prototype used this way is not a waste. It is one of the best things these tools do. You can see a contradiction on Tuesday that used to wait until the build was too far along to argue with. I would not give that up.

The trouble starts when polish is mistaken for proof.

A wrong assumption that has been rendered, permissioned, and demoed looks like a decision. The system behaves, so the guess that produced it starts to feel settled. I have felt that shift in the room. Once you can click the thing, objecting to the premise feels like objecting to working software.

That also moves authority. A small group that can make an imagined solution tangible this week will shape the product more than a larger group still waiting on evidence. Being first to a runnable version is not the same as being right about what should run. And if that version is then implemented in a system people depend on, the untested assumption stops being a model. It becomes a commitment.

Compression does not supply the missing knowledge

Some of the old weaknesses of waterfall really do shrink. A specification that used to stay abstract can be exercised. Contradictions surface early. Feedback does not wait on a quarter. That is a genuine gain, and it is easy to overread.

A prototype refined by the people collectively imagining how it should work can become extraordinarily complete and still leave the important question untouched. Waterfall's deeper problem was never only that the document was slow to test. It was that we did not yet know what people needed. A faster, clickable specification does not automatically answer that. It can make the gap harder to see, because the assumption already runs.

For a long time, engineering effort put a crude limit on how quickly an assumption could become a commitment. These tools weaken that limit. But making an assumption executable doesn't make it any more likely to be correct.

The problem isn't prototyping. It's what happens when iteration within a prototype becomes a substitute for testing its underlying assumptions. We've become extraordinarily good at rapid iteration. But we may also be smuggling waterfall back into the process under cover of that very speed.

And that's the irony. The same tools that let us perfect a preconceived solution could instead make it dramatically easier to challenge that solution: to put something simple in front of real users, observe what happens, and change direction.

We've spent decades learning that we shouldn't design the entire solution before putting it in front of users. Now we can do exactly that, only much faster.

Are we using AI to become more agile, or to make waterfall so efficient that we forget why we abandoned it?
